# The map drawer

The cabinet had stood in the back room of the shop for as long as
anyone could remember, and everyone — Hannah included — had
treated it as part of the building, like the radiator that
clanked in winter or the warped third step on the stairs to the
flat above. It was very tall, and very deep, and it had nine
shallow drawers, each of which slid out with a sound like a long
held breath being released, and each of which contained maps.

The maps were of nowhere in particular. That is to say, they
were of places, certainly, and the places had names, but the
names did not seem to belong to a single coherent geography. One
drawer held a hand-tinted survey of the marshland east of a
river called the Wenny, which Hannah had never heard of and
could not find in any atlas; another contained a series of
ordnance-style sheets covering an archipelago whose largest
island bore a name that sounded faintly Welsh and faintly Greek
at once. A third drawer was full of nothing but railway
timetables, each printed on tissue paper so thin she could see
her fingertips through it, and each describing services that
appeared to run between stations whose names she did not
recognise on routes whose lengths could not be reconciled with
any map she had ever seen.

The cabinet had been her grandfather's, which is to say that he
had owned the shop and the cabinet had been in the back room
when he died, and now the shop was hers and the cabinet was hers
and the maps, presumably, were hers as well. She had never
opened any of the drawers in the years she had worked alongside
him, partly because the back room was always full of the things
that the front room could not hold, and partly because her
grandfather, who had a great many opinions and was not shy of
voicing them, had been quiet on the subject of the cabinet in a
way that suggested he preferred her to be quiet about it too.

After he died, she had not gone to the cabinet immediately. She
had sorted through the receipt drawer in the till, which
contained nothing of interest; she had been through the small
filing cabinet under the desk, which contained the predictable
quantity of supplier correspondence and tax forms; she had made
the predictable arrangements with the lawyer and the council and
the man from the bank. She had wept, twice, in the predictable
places. It was only after some weeks, when she found herself
alone in the shop on a quiet Tuesday afternoon and could think of
nothing in particular that needed doing, that she went to the
back room and stood in front of the cabinet for a long time
without opening it.

The room smelled of cardboard and dust and, faintly, of the
peppermints her grandfather had kept in his pocket. The light
came in through the high window above the cabinet and fell on
the wood in a way that made her think of churches, although she
had no religion to speak of and her grandfather had not been a
churchgoer either. After a while she put her hand on the brass
pull of the topmost drawer, which felt cold and heavy in a way
she had not expected, and she drew the drawer out.

Inside the drawer there was a single map, much larger than the
drawer should have been able to contain, folded into a precise
square so that it filled the drawer's bottom exactly. The map
was on a paper she did not recognise; it was thicker than tissue
and thinner than parchment, and it had the slightly waxy feel
of something that had been treated to last. The folds were
sharp and clean, as if someone had folded it carefully and then
not opened it again for a very long time, but the paper was not
yellowed, and the ink — black, with sparing applications of
ochre and a single, surprising line of green — was as fresh as
if it had been put down that morning.

She did not unfold the map. She looked at it for some minutes,
and then she closed the drawer, and then she stood there in
front of the closed drawer and tried to decide whether what she
had seen was something she had wanted to see. She thought, on
balance, that it was not, but she also thought that she was
going to come back and look at it again, and she would have liked
to know what to expect when she did.

Outside, the afternoon was beginning to give up. The shop was
silent, as it had been for most of the time she had owned it.
The bell over the front door, which had not rung in three hours,
did not ring now, and Hannah, who had felt for some weeks that
her grandfather's death had taken something away that she could
not name, began to wonder for the first time whether the cabinet
had been the something, and whether opening it now had started
the long slow business of getting it back.

She turned away from the cabinet and went back into the front
of the shop. The light in there was different — grey and
ordinary — and the dust motes that had been visible above the
cabinet were not visible here. She made a cup of tea from the
electric kettle that lived under the counter, and she sat on
the stool by the till, and she drank the tea, and she thought
about the map.

In the days that followed she did open more of the drawers,
though she did so cautiously and never more than one at a time.
The marshland east of the Wenny was the second drawer down. The
archipelago was the fifth. The tissue-paper timetables were the
seventh. There were drawers she had not yet opened, and would
not open for some time, and the question of what was in those
drawers became, by degrees, the only question that mattered.
