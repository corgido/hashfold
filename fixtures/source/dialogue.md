# The kitchen table

"You're sure about this," she said, not as a question.

"I'm sure."

"Because last time you were sure, we ended up with the Williams
account, and you remember how that went."

He folded the paper in half and then in half again, watching the
crease line up. "That was different."

"Different how?"

"They wanted us to lie. This is just—" he gestured vaguely,
"—careful."

"Careful." She put down her coffee cup. "Careful is what people
say when they want permission to do the thing they were going to
do anyway."

"That's not fair."

"It's not meant to be fair. It's meant to be accurate."

He didn't answer for a long moment. The kettle, which had been
about to whistle for some time, finally did, and she got up to
deal with it. He watched her cross to the counter, pour the
water, return. She didn't ask if he wanted anything.

"Look," he said eventually. "I'm not trying to win an argument.
I'm trying to tell you what I'm going to do."

"I know what you're going to do. You're going to call them back
and say yes, and then you're going to spend the next three months
telling yourself you had to."

"Maybe I did have to."

"Maybe. But you'd be telling yourself either way, wouldn't you."

He almost laughed. It wasn't a kind laugh, but it wasn't unkind
either; it was the laugh of someone who has been seen and is
trying to decide whether to be embarrassed or grateful.

"Why are we even talking about this?" he asked. "It's my call.
You said it was my call."

"I said it was your decision. I didn't say I had to keep my
opinions to myself while you made it."

"What's the difference?"

"There's all the difference in the world. A decision is
something you do. An opinion is something I have. They don't
cancel out."

He looked at her for a long time then. There was something in
her face he didn't entirely recognise — not anger, not
disappointment, something more patient than either of those.

"Did you ever want me to choose differently?" he said. "Before,
I mean. When I was deciding things."

"Sometimes."

"Why didn't you say so?"

"I did say so. You weren't always listening."

He thought about that. "Was I listening this time?"

"This time you came and asked me. So yes. Probably."

"And?"

"And I told you what I think. The rest is yours."

He picked up his own cup, which had gone cold while they talked,
and put it down again without drinking.

"I'm going to call them in the morning," he said.

"All right."

"You think I'm wrong."

"I think you might be wrong. I don't think you're wrong because
you're you and I'm me and we don't have to agree on this."

"That's a very generous way of putting it."

"It's not generous," she said. "It's just true."

Outside, somewhere down the street, a car door slammed and an
engine started. He counted slowly to ten in his head, the way
he had done as a child when he wanted to make a moment last.

"Okay," he said. "Okay."

She didn't say anything to that. After a while she got up and
took the kettle off the stove and put it back on the cold burner,
and after another while she came back and sat down across from
him, and they sat like that for some time, not talking, while
the morning went on outside.
