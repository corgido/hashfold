The study examined three competing hypotheses about urban migration patterns. However, the data revealed unexpected complexity that none of the original models had predicted. Consequently, the researchers were forced to revise their analytical framework before proceeding.

The first hypothesis proposed that economic factors dominate migration decisions. But the survey data told a different story. While income differentials correlated with movement between regions, they failed to explain intra-city relocation patterns. Furthermore, when controlling for housing costs, the economic signal weakened considerably. Therefore, a purely economic model was insufficient.

The second hypothesis emphasised social networks. Since migrants tend to settle near existing community members, this model predicted clustering patterns. Indeed, the spatial analysis confirmed significant clustering in three of the five cities studied. Nevertheless, the two outlier cities showed dispersal patterns that contradicted the network hypothesis. Moreover, even in the clustering cities, second-generation residents showed markedly different spatial behaviour. Thus, social networks explained initial settlement but not long-term distribution.

The third hypothesis focused on institutional factors — specifically, housing policy and zoning regulations. Although this model had received less attention in the literature, the evidence proved surprisingly strong. For instance, cities with inclusionary zoning showed more dispersed settlement patterns regardless of economic conditions or network effects. In contrast, cities with restrictive land-use policies concentrated migrants in predictable corridors. Accordingly, institutional design appeared to shape outcomes more powerfully than individual decision-making.

However, none of these hypotheses operated in isolation. Rather, the interaction effects were substantial. Since economic incentives channel migrants toward affordable areas, and since zoning determines where affordable housing exists, the economic and institutional factors were deeply entangled. Similarly, network effects reinforced institutional patterns because early settlers attracted later arrivals to the same policy-shaped corridors.

The researchers therefore proposed an integrated model. Instead of treating the three factors as competing explanations, they modelled them as interacting layers. Consequently, the revised framework explained significantly more variance than any single-factor model. Nevertheless, roughly eighteen percent of the observed variation remained unexplained, suggesting that additional factors — perhaps cultural or psychological — also played a role.

In conclusion, the study demonstrated that migration patterns resist simple causal narratives. While each hypothesis captured a genuine mechanism, none was sufficient alone. Furthermore, the interaction effects meant that policy interventions targeting only one factor would likely produce smaller effects than expected. Therefore, effective policy requires coordinated action across economic, social, and institutional dimensions simultaneously.
